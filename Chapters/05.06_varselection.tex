\section*{Summary}\label{abstract}
\textbf{Objective}: Variable selection (VS) for clinical prediction models is usually performed on statistical grounds, for example using information criteria or p-values. These approaches ignore the cost of misclassification and the cost of measuring predictors. We propose a decision-analytic variable-selection method based on net benefit and implement it in the open-source \texttt{NBvarsel} R package. \\
\textbf{Materials and Methods}: The proposed algorithm selects predictors by exhaustively comparing the net benefit of all candidate subsets using cross-validation, with an extension based on backward elimination for high-dimensional settings. It provides variable-importance measures as the contribution of each predictor to utility and allows the user to specify the cost of measuring a predictor. We illustrate the package with a simulation study and three real case studies, evaluating discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. \\
\textbf{Results}: Across the three case studies, \texttt{NBvarsel} produced more parsimonious models that maintained or improved clinical utility with minimal loss of discrimination and calibration. If costs of measuring predictors are considered, the method tends to exclude costly predictors that contribute little to utility. \\
\textbf{Discussion}: \texttt{NBvarsel} is a promising approach for integrating clinical utility directly into variable selection, allowing prediction models to maximize patient benefit while minimizing unnecessary clinical burden. \\
\textbf{Conclusion}: We present an easy-to-use algorithm based on the utility that a model would have in the clinic to select the best subset of predictors, which leads to simpler models maintaining the same utility.

\section{Introduction}

Determining a patient's condition based on their current characteristics is called diagnosis; it is key to initiating treatment and minimizing future complications. Different conditions require assessing different characteristics: for example, a bone fracture can be diagnosed using medical imaging, while COVID-19 can be diagnosed using a PCR test. Clinical prediction models are statistical tools that estimate the probability that a patient has, or will develop, a condition in the future \cite{steyerberg2019clinical,jamesIntroductionStatisticalLearning2021}. These models link the outcome (event or condition) to a set of predictors, a task often called supervised learning. Predictors should be information readily available at the time of diagnosis, not too costly to measure, and measured with reasonable precision \cite{steyerberg2019clinical}. However, many predictors may meet these criteria, and selecting among them is therefore necessary \cite{chowdhuryVariableSelectionStrategies2020}.

Variable selection, best subset selection, feature selection, and feature engineering are terms used in prediction modelling to describe the process of selecting which predictors to use when training a model. Different approaches for variable selection have been presented. Heinze and colleagues \cite{heinzeVariableSelectionReview2018} categorized methods into five groups based on the criteria to select the variables: (1) based on significance testing, (2) based on information criteria (e.g.\ AIC, BIC), (3) based on penalized likelihood (e.g.\ LASSO, elastic-net), (4) based on change-in-estimate or performance (e.g.\ backward elimination, stepwise selection), or (5) based on background or domain knowledge. Across machine learning literature, it is often observed that variable selection methods are grouped into filter, wrapper and embedded methods \cite{Nguyen2025Vol1}. Filter methods use statistical properties to select variables and are therefore equivalent to significance testing and information criteria-based approaches. Wrapper methods, in contrast, use model performance to select the best subset, which corresponds to Heinze's change-in-estimate criterion. Finally, embedded methods perform variable selection as part of the model training; this includes penalized likelihood but also other approaches such as feature importance calculation in tree-based or genetic algorithms \cite{archerEmpiricalCharacterizationRandom2008,zhangVariableSelectionLogistic2018}. This work will focus on change-in-estimate or wrapper methods for variable selection in clinical prediction models.

To be used in clinical practice, prediction models need to show good performance in external validation in terms of discrimination (area under the ROC curve), calibration (calibration plot), and clinical utility (\gls{NB}) \cite{calsterPerformanceEvaluationPredictive2024}. Variable selection is usually performed by considering only the statistical properties of the predictors (filter methods), without accounting for the clinical benefit or utility. Utility refers to the quality of decision-making when a risk model is used to decide whether a patient requires treatment \cite{vancalsterReportingInterpretingDecision2018}. Models that are not clinically useful are more likely to be forgotten quickly or even never be implemented in real practice \cite{wyattCommentaryPrognosticModels1995}. Consequently, we aim to include clinical utility in the model development process through a clinical utility guided variable selection.

Utility values, relating to misclassification costs (i.e., the benefit of treating the diseased and the harm of treating the healthy), are hard to elicit at an absolute scale. However, it is known that the ratio of harm to benefit is directly related to the decision threshold for classifying a patient as high risk \cite{vancalsterReportingInterpretingDecision2018}. \gls{NB}, a simple metric for clinical utility, uses this relationship. \Gls{NB} is calculated as benefit minus harm multiplied by the harm-to-benefit ratio \cite{vickersDecisionCurveAnalysis2006}. In statistical terms, this is the number of true positives minus the number of false positives weighted by the harm-to-benefit ratio. This ratio reflects how many false positives a clinician is willing to accept per true positive. For example, if a surgeon is willing to perform 9 surgeries on benign tumors to catch 1 malignant tumor, the exchange rate is 1/9, corresponding to a 10\% decision threshold. However, \gls{NB} should often be presented across a range of thresholds, given variability across clinicians and settings \cite{vickersSimpleStepbystepGuide2019}. The use of \gls{NB} and related metrics to evaluate a new variable was discussed by Baker and colleagues \cite{bakerEvaluatingNewMarker2012}, but it has never been implemented as a variable selection algorithm for clinical prediction models.

The aim of this paper is therefore to develop a simple and interpretable variable selection algorithm based on clinical utility to determine which predictors to include in the model and implement it as an easy to use R package. Additionally, we will explore the effect on performance of the method. We will use and implement the method in the context of a logistic regression to estimate the probability of a binary outcome, but the rationale extends to more flexible machine learning or AI probabilistic algorithms.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Algorithm}
\label{sec:algorithm}
The problem we aim to address is defined as follows: given a set of predictors $\mathbf{P}$ and a modelling algorithm $\mathbf{M}$ we seek the subset of $\mathbf{P}$ that maximises the net benefit:

\begin{equation}
\underset{\mathbf{S} \subseteq \mathbf{P}}{\operatorname{argmax}} \widehat{NB}(\mathbf{S}, \mathbf{M}, t)
\end{equation}

Here, $\widehat{NB}(\mathbf{S}, \mathbf{M}, t)$ denotes the (adjusted) net benefit of algorithm $\mathbf{M}$ with $\mathbf{S}$ predictors for the decision threshold $t$. The $t$ parameter may be a single value, or a range of clinically relevant values specified based on clinical context. In cases where a range of values is used, we want to maximize the average \gls{NB} across that range. This is the general rationale for the methodology; however, we will focus here on the specific case where a logistic regression model is used as the modelling algorithm.

The proposed variable-selection algorithm does not modify coefficient estimation, since log-loss minimisation (or log-likelihood maximisation) provides coefficients with desirable properties. The algorithm proceeds as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Define all possible model combinations based on the predictors
\item Assign a test tradeoff (harm) to each predictor or group of predictors to adjust the \gls{NB} accordingly. If the harm is unknown, it can be set to 0 and the unadjusted \gls{NB} will be used.
\item Train models and evaluate their net benefit using cross-validation (default: 5 folds).
\begin{itemize}
\item This step can be parallelized to reduce computation time.
\item Nonlinearities can be modelled with restricted cubic splines.
\item Interactions can be included; we limit this option to either all pairwise interactions or no interactions.
\end{itemize}
\item Rank the models based on \gls{NB} and select the model with optimal \gls{NB}.
\end{enumerate}

Code implementing the Net Benefit based variable selection and the associated visualizations is available in the open-source R package \texttt{NBvarsel} (\url{https://lasaibarrenada.github.io/NB_varsel/}, Box~1).

\subsection{Groupwise net benefit variable selection}

The algorithm presented in Section~\ref{sec:algorithm} becomes computationally too demanding when the number of candidate predictors increases. For a dataset with 20 candidate predictors, there are more than one million predictor combinations; consequently, the method fits 5 million models when using 5-fold cross-validation. In most trials or observational studies, the sample size is limited; therefore, the number of candidate predictors should also be kept small. Our methodology should always be used in compliance with these sample size criteria as explained in Section~\ref{sec:sample_size}. However, in those cases where the data are high-dimensional (e.g. electronic health records), we propose an extension of the methodology that we call groupwise (as opposed to exhaustive) \gls{NB} variable selection.

In this extension, instead of calculating all the possible combinations, we use backward elimination with a number of predictors per step specified ($k$), yielding a total of $\binom{p}{k}$ model combinations per step. The fastest version ($k = 1$) therefore would fit $p$ models by removing a single predictor in the first iteration. Then the model with best performance is selected, if that model is the model where all predictors are used, that is selected as the best model, else the predictor(s) that were removed in the model with best performance are removed and the process is iteratively repeated until reaching the optimal model. Allowing values of $k$ higher than one is highly recommended if there is intuition of important interaction effects between predictors.

\subsection{Note on Sample size}
\label{sec:sample_size}
Across systematic reviews of prediction model development and validation studies, a common issue is that there is no rationale for the sample size used \cite{barrenadaADNEXRiskPrediction2024,wynantsPredictionModelsDiagnosis2020}. International guidelines for prediction modelling, such as TRIPOD+AI and PROBAST+AI, recommend always justifying sample size \cite{collinsTRIPODAIStatementUpdated2024,moonsPROBASTAIUpdatedQuality2025}.

The ratio of the number of events and potential number of predictors is called \gls{EPV} or \gls{EPP}. We prefer the latter because it explicitly states that its value should always be based on potential predictors, prior to data-driven variable selection; otherwise, the coefficients will be biased  \cite{steyerbergLogisticRegressionModeling2011}. In traditional logistic and linear regression, \gls{EPP} indicates relationship between the number of coefficients to be estimated and the amount of available information. The first recommendations indicated that an \gls{EPP} of greater than ten was adequate \cite{concatoImportanceEventsIndependent1995,peduzziSimulationStudyNumber1996}. Some years later, several authors disputed this rule, concluding that there is no single \gls{EPP} rule that would guarantee accurate estimation \cite{vittinghoffRelaxingRuleTen2007,courvoisierPerformanceLogisticRegression2011,vansmedenNoRationale12016}.

Moving beyond rules of thumb, Riley and colleagues have developed several minimum sample size calculation techniques for developing and validating multivariable binary, continuous and time-to-event models \cite{archerMinimumSampleSize2021,pateMinimumSampleSize2023,rileyMinimumSampleSize2022,rileyEvaluationClinicalPrediction2024a,rileyMinimumSampleSize2019,rileyMinimumSampleSize2019a}. Recently, the group has published a general framework that applies both to linear models and to more flexible AI-based algorithms such as random forests \cite{rileyGeneralSampleSize2026}, that had shown to be more ``data hungry'' \cite{austinRelativeDataHungriness2024,vanderploegModernModellingTechniques2014}. These criteria are based on overfitting and optimism control and precise estimation of the intercept.

When sample data is limited, a two-stage approach is often recommended: first selecting a subset of predictors based on subject matter knowledge and then performing a data-driven variable selection \cite{harrellRegressionModelingStrategies2015,steyerberg2019clinical}. Because it is prone to overfitting like any data-driven process, the methodology presented here must always adhere to the recommended minimum sample size.

\subsection{Test harms}

The concept of placing benefit and harm on the same scale is the principle of \gls{NB} and \gls{DCA}, yet it has not been applied directly to variable selection. In this work, we aim to introduce this concept in model development by defining a harm adjusted \gls{NB}. Harm is therefore accounted for twice. First, to calculate \gls{NB}, we set a threshold that should be related to the test harm (harm of misclassifying a patient). Second, the harm of measuring a predictor is introduced in the \gls{NB} scale by quantifying the minimal expected benefit required to measure a variable (Box 2). The harm of misclassifying a patient is widely discussed in the literature \cite{vickersSimpleStepbystepGuide2019,vickersDecisionCurveAnalysis2006}. The harm of measuring a predictor can be quantified as the number of tests that a doctor is willing to perform to find an event, also known as \gls{TT} \cite{vancalsterReportingInterpretingDecision2018}. If the number is higher, then the cost of the predictor is lower. However, specifying this number upfront might be difficult, therefore, the difference in \gls{NB} ($\Delta \gls{NB}$) can be used to derive the \gls{TT}. \gls{TT} is defined as:

\begin{equation}
TT = \frac{\Delta NB}{q \cdot (1-q)}
\end{equation}

where $\Delta NB$ is the \gls{NB} difference between the model with the additional predictor and the model without the predictor. Imagine we have a model that predicts risk of ovarian cancer with age and ultrasound measurements of the lesion that has a \gls{NB} of 0.2. We wonder whether measuring CA125, which requires a blood sample, and including it in the model is cost-effective from the \gls{NB} perspective. Given the monetary costs and test burden, practitioners suggest they would be willing to measure CA125 in 100 patients to find an event. This implies that we would include the predictor only if the \gls{NB} increases by at least 0.01 ($\Delta \gls{NB} > 0.01$). By doing so, the researcher can express harm in terms of how many tests per true positive are needed to justify inclusion of that predictor. For some predictors, such as age or sex, the harm is minimal and the TT can therefore be high, meaning that almost any improvement justifies their inclusion. Other predictors may have a higher associated harm (for example, a CT scan, which involves monetary costs, discomfort, and exposure to radiation). Harmful predictors have low TT; therefore the expected increase in utility must be larger. Quantifying these harms can be difficult and will affect model selection. Some predictors may have shared harms; for instance, a single blood sample could provide information on several predictors. In those cases, the harm attributed to one or to all of those predictors is identical, and the adjusted \gls{NB} is calculated accordingly. If harms are omitted, variable selection is based solely on standard \gls{NB}.

\begin{tcolorbox}[title={Box 2: Harm of a test and test tradeoff}, label={box:harm}]
We acknowledge that determining the harm of measuring a predictor in the context of a model development study is challenging. In this box we propose some aspects to consider when determining the harms.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Harms are broad:} They go beyond monetary costs and include patient burden, potential side effects, objectivity of the measurement, need for specific equipment, time required, and other factors.
    \item \textbf{Harm for the model $\\neq$ Harm of the test:} A predictor might be costly at a population level but, in the context of a prediction problem, be readily available because it is measured routinely.
    \item \textbf{Harms are context-dependent:} Harms depend on the clinical context and setting and should be defined accordingly.
    \item \textbf{Combined harms:} Some tests may cause harm but provide information for several predictors. In those cases, the harm attributed to one or to all of those predictors should be treated consistently.
\end{itemize}

In the algorithm presented, the harms should be defined according to these aspects. For that purpose, we propose the \textbf{test tradeoff}. This can facilitate setting harms by determining them as the number of times a clinician would be willing to measure a predictor to find a new case. If used, the tradeoff should be defined carefully by experts before inspecting the data.

For most cases where the harms cannot be clearly defined, we advise researchers not to use the harms and to select variables based on \gls{NB} and analyse the contribution of each predictor to the total \gls{NB}.
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsection{Variable importance}

When the test tradeoff or $TT$ is difficult to quantify, an alternative approach to guide variable inclusion is to compute each variable's contribution to clinical utility. Variable importance is typically assessed using the statistical properties of the model being analysed. For instance, in a logistic regression model, coefficients can be used to compare the relative contribution of each predictor, while in random-forest models the Gini importance is often used to rank predictors by their contribution to impurity reduction. Other model-agnostic procedures have been proposed in the explainable AI field \cite{dwivediExplainableAIXAI2023}, with SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values and Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME) among the most popular \cite{lundbergUnifiedApproachInterpreting2017,ribeiroWhyShouldTrust2016}. However, these techniques assess variable importance based on predicted probabilities rather than on clinical utility.

In his seminal paper introducing random forests, Breiman introduced permutation variable importance \cite{breimanRandomForests2001}. The algorithm proceeds as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A model is trained on training data
\item The performance of the model is evaluated on test or unobserved data
\item The performance of the model on test data is recalculated by randomly shuffling (permuting) values of a predictor $P$
\item The variable importance of $P$ is the difference between the performance on the true dataset and on the permuted dataset
\end{enumerate}

We apply this approach to calculate the average contribution of each predictor to the Net Benefit across the different predictor combinations evaluated. The permuted performance is calculated within the same $k$ folds of the cross-validation to ensure that the permuted and original performances are comparable. It is important to note that this variable importance is the average across all evaluated predictor combinations; therefore, the results might be distorted due to unrealistic combinations. For a full picture we provide the contribution per scenario so that the contribution per predictor could also be calculated in a subset of scenarios.

\subsection{Simple illustration}
\label{sec:simple_il}
 We illustrate the method with a simple simulated example. Consider four predictors, two continuous ($X_1, X_3$) and two binary ($X_2, X_4$), used to predict a binary outcome $Y$ generated from:

 \begin{equation}
 	logit(P(Y=1)) = -3 + 3X_1 + 2X_2 + 0.1X_3 - 0.05X_4
 \end{equation}

 In this scenario, $X_1$ and $X_2$ are strongly predictive while $X_3$ and $X_4$ are weak predictors; the outcome prevalence is 32\%. Each predictor has an associated test harm: $X_1$ and $X_2$ (0.05, TT = 20; high), $X_3$ (0.025, TT = 40; moderate), and $X_4$ (0.00005, TT = 20000; marginal). We simulated 1000 observations and trained models using several variable selection procedures: backward elimination (BE, via \textit{fastbw()} in \texttt{rms}), net-benefit variable selection (\texttt{NBvarsel}), and the best-performing subset by Brier score and AUC.

 The BE model retained $X_1$ and $X_2$, the strongest predictors. \texttt{NBvarsel} retained $X_1$ and $X_4$, reflecting the effect of test harms. If harms were ignored, the best model by performance would have included $X_1$, $X_2$, and $X_3$, excluding $X_4$. Figure~\ref{fig:varsel:importanceA} shows variable importance: $X_1$ and $X_2$ are the main contributors to utility, which aligns with the observation that the full model and the model with only $X_1$ and $X_2$ achieve similar \gls{NB}. When specifying harms is difficult, variable-importance summaries can guide selection. The all-subset plot (Figure~\ref{fig:varsel:importanceB}) displays the average adjusted or unadjusted \gls{NB} across combinations with a reference to the maximum \gls{NB}; it provides a visual explanation of each variable's contribution but becomes impractical to display as the number of predictors grows.

\section{Case studies}

We will test the variable selection methodology (\texttt{NBvarsel}) with three datasets previously used to develop clinical prediction models. We will then compare the performance in terms of discrimination (\gls{AUC}), calibration (calibration plots and \gls{OE}) and clinical utility (\gls{NB}  and \gls{DCA}). All the analyses were performed using R version 4.4. We share complete R code to replicate all the results presented in this paper in the OSF repository, although for the ovarian cancer and non-typhoidal salmonella, data is not publicly available.

\subsection{Ovarian cancer diagnosis -- IOTA data}

The optimal management of patients with an ovarian tumour depends on the histology of the mass. Benign masses do not always need to be removed and can be monitored with ultrasound follow up \cite{carleyLaparoscopyLaparotomyManagement2002}. According to a recent study with 2 year follow-up, most of these masses spontaneously resolve, which reduces the risk of surgical complications \cite{froymanRiskComplicationsPatients2019}. If the mass is malignant, management in specialised oncology centers is suggested \cite{timmermanESGOISUOGIOTA2021}. The IOTA group has developed and implemented several prediction models that use ultrasound and clinical characteristics to estimate the risk of malignancy of ovarian masses preoperatively \cite{kaijserImprovingStrategiesDiagnosing2013}. In 2014, the ADNEX model was published using data from the IOTA phases 1-3 (N = 5909, events = 1929) \cite{vancalsterEvaluatingRiskOvarian2014}. The model has shown excellent performance across different settings and populations, as shown in recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses \cite{barrenadaADNEXRiskPrediction2024,barrenadaHeadHeadComparison2025}. ADNEX is a multinomial model that predicts the risk of benign, borderline, stage I invasive cancer, stage II-IV invasive cancer or metastatic cancer. In this work, we will treat it as a binary model, where the risk of malignancy is equal to the sum of the four malignant subtypes. As a validation dataset we will use IOTA phase 5 study (N = 6847, events = 1713) which was prospectively collected to externally validate ADNEX \cite{vancalsterValidationModelsDiagnose2020}.

The variable selection procedure is detailed in the supplementary material of the original publication \cite{vancalsterEvaluatingRiskOvarian2014}, we refer the reader there for details. The original \gls{ADNEX} model utilized nine predictors: three clinical (age, serum \gls{CA125}, and center type) and six ultrasound-based (lesion diameter, solid tissue proportion, >10 cyst locules, number of papillary projections, acoustic shadows, and ascites). While family history was initially considered, it was excluded during the variable selection process. For our analysis, missing \gls{CA125} values were handled via a regression-based imputation using all other \gls{ADNEX} predictors, following recent methodological recommendations \cite{siskImputationMissingIndicators2023}. Then this model was applied to the development and validation dataset to impute missing values. In the validation dataset, some of the tumours were classified as uncertain for various reasons. In the published validation study, these values were also imputed with multiple imputation. We use one set of those imputed values for our analysis.

We extended the original predictor set with five additional features: bilateral masses (Yes/No), irregular internal cyst walls (Yes/No), Doppler color score (4 levels), maximum solid component diameter, and lesion-associated pain (Yes/No). The distribution of predictors by outcome for the training and test dataset is included in Table S2. In total 65,536 predictor combinations were evaluated across decision thresholds from 1\% to 20\% risk with 20-fold cross-validation. This means that the average \gls{NB} across those thresholds is the metric to optimize. We then computed discrimination in terms of AUC, O:E ratio, clustered calibration and DCA \cite{barrenadaClusteredFlexibleCalibration2026}. Adjusted \gls{NB} was also calculated with harms based on three groups of variables: clinical-history variables (no cost; typically available in the record), ultrasound-based variables (TT = 200), and blood-based variables (TT = 80). We compared \gls{NB}-based variable selection to LASSO and backward elimination.

\subsubsection{Results}

All models removed family history of ovarian cancer. The LASSO model also removed presence of papillary projections. \texttt{NBvarsel} models removed instead maximum diameter of the solid part of the tumour and in addition the adjusted \texttt{NBvarsel} removed CA125 biomarker. According to the variable importance calculations, the least important predictor was family history, and the most important one was the proportion of solid tissue (Figure S2).

The models showed comparable performance, with BE and \texttt{NBvarsel} approaches outperforming slightly the rest (Table \ref{tab:varsel:iota}). All models tended to underestimate the risk of malignancy as shown in the clustered calibration plots with the backward elimination method yielding slightly closer curve to the diagonal (Figure S3). Clinical utility of all models was comparable except for ADNEX with CA125 and Lasso that showed slightly lower utility, as shown in the decision curves (Figure \ref{fig:varsel:iota}, left), but when the costs of measuring a predictor are accounted for, the \texttt{NBvarsel} adjusted and ADNEX without CA125 had higher \gls{NB} across the whole range of decision thresholds due to removing the costly CA125 predictor (Figure \ref{fig:varsel:iota}, right). All-subset plots are available in Supplementary material (Figures S4-S5).

\subsection{Emergency department hospitalization -- MIMIC IV ED}

For the second case study we used the MIMIC IV emergency department (MIMIC IV ED) data to predict the risk of hospitalization following an ED visit \cite{PhysioNet-mimiciv-3.1,PhysioNet-mimic-iv-ed-2.2,johnsonMIMICIVFreelyAccessible2023}. MIMIC IV ED contains electronic health record data from more than 425,000 ED stays at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (2011--2019). We followed the benchmark proposed by Xie et al., updated to MIMIC IV v3.1 and MIMIC ED v2.2 \cite{xieBenchmarkingEmergencyDepartment2022}, and used their recommended procedure to obtain training and test splits (training: 334,420 patients, 80\%; testing: 83,605 patients, 20\%).

The benchmark defines 64 candidate predictors for hospitalization. We focus on a baseline logistic regression model and compare it with groupwise \gls{NB} variable selection. Hospitalization is defined as admission to an inpatient unit immediately after the ED visit; the prevalence in the dataset was 47\%. The predictor distributions in the training and test sets are shown in Table S3. An exhaustive search over all predictor subsets would entail approximately $2^{64}$ combinations, which is infeasible. Therefore we applied a groupwise approach designed for high-dimensional settings with group sizes 1 and 2. We ran the algorithm with 5 cross-validation folds and selected variables based on the average \gls{NB} across the full range of decision thresholds. The benchmark authors selected an operating point at the ROC curve nearest the upper-left corner (0.446); however, defining a single threshold from the data is not recommended \cite{onbehalfofthetopicgroupevaluatingdiagnostictestsandpredictionmodelsofthestratosinitiativeThreeMythsRisk2019}. We therefore evaluated performance across the whole threshold range to reflect how hospitalization thresholds vary with clinical context and resources.

\subsubsection{Results}

The algorithm with group size = 1 trains 65 models, followed by 64 in the next step, and so on. For MIMIC IV-ED dataset, 8 predictors were removed using this algorithm and the process took 1.45 hours. The method with group size of two iteratively removes predictors in pairs, so it accounts for interactions of two predictors. This method is computationally more intensive because the algorithm needs to train 2017 models in first step, followed by 1953 and so on. In the data, the algorithm removed 12 predictors in 6 steps.

The most important predictors according to \texttt{NBvarsel} were triage acuity (Emergency Severity Index) and age of the patient (Figure S6). The most important variables for \texttt{NBvarsel} were similar to those reported in the original study calculated using RF \cite{xieBenchmarkingEmergencyDepartment2022}. Baseline model and groupwise approaches obtained similar AUC (0.81). Calibration was slightly closer to the diagonal for the groupwise variable selection but tended to overestimate high risk more than the baseline approach (Figure S7). \texttt{NBvarsel} models consistently ranked slightly higher for clinical utility across the decision curve except for very high thresholds where baseline was more useful (Table~\ref{tab:varsel:mimic}, Figure~\ref{fig:varsel:mimic}).

\subsection{Non typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) - DeNTS trial}

The last case study will use prospective cohort data to predict the risk of non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) bloodstream infection in children under five to guide the choice of empirical antibiotics \cite{tackDevelopingClinicalPrediction2025}. The dataset contains clinical information collected prospectively from 2,327 paediatric hospital admissions with severe febrile illness at the Kisantu district hospital in the Democratic Republic of Congo (Table S4). We use the a priori clinical predictors proposed by Tack et al., applying their exact variable definitions and restricted cubic spline transformations for continuous predictors. The authors propose this specific set of predictors based on a systematic literature review to ensure clinical relevance and avoid data-driven bias; therefore, we followed the same procedure to obtain our variables according to their guidelines. They propose 11 predictors to develop models for antibiotic triage, and they develop a simplified clinical scoring system alongside it, but we will focus on their main multivariable logistic regression (LR) model.

In their work, variables were selected using a double backward elimination process and the final model was fitted using Firth's correction \cite{puhrFirthsLogisticRegression2017}. We compare that approach to the proposed net-benefit variable selection, allowing splines for continuous predictors and fitting the final model with Firth's correction. In our algorithm, continuous predictors were either modelled with splines or removed. The authors recommended a reasonable threshold range between 20\% and 50\%; we used this range in the variable selection procedure.

If we were to use the exhaustive variable-selection approach as in previous case studies, we would evaluate 2,047 combinations. No test data were available; therefore, we present apparent results first and then correct for optimism by comparing the published approach and our algorithm across 1000 bootstrap samples. The bootstrap included the \gls{NB}-based variable selection and the published variable selection.

\subsubsection{Apparent performance}

According to \texttt{NBvarsel}, the model removed age, nutritional status and haemoglobin levels and the most important predictors were malaria status, duration of fever and respiratory rate (Figures S8-S9). Individual risks varied considerably among the published model and the \texttt{NBvarsel} model (Figure~\ref{fig:varsel:dents}). AUC was slightly higher for the published model (0.80 vs 0.79). The calibration of the model was closer to the diagonal for the \texttt{NBvarsel} model for estimated risk below 45\% but tended to estimate too low risks in higher estimated probabilities (Figure S10). The \texttt{NBvarsel} model had higher \gls{NB} from decision thresholds of 25\% till 55\% (Figure S11).

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Summary of Key Findings}

While classical statistical properties are widely used to build clinical prediction models, relying solely on them for variable selection often fails to optimise clinical benefit and to account for the real-world burden of measuring predictors. To address this gap, we developed and implemented a variable selection algorithm as an easy-to-use R package that determines the optimal subset of predictors by exhaustively exploring all predictor combinations and maximizing clinical utility, measured via harm-adjusted or traditional \gls{NB}. Through our simple illustration and three case studies, we showed that this decision analysis-based approach effectively reduces model complexity, maintaining comparable discrimination, calibration and clinical utility. In the ovarian cancer case study, adjusting for measurement harm successfully removed the CA125 biomarker, a test requiring a blood draw, while maintaining comparable performance to traditional backward elimination and LASSO methods. Furthermore, we showed that by using a groupwise elimination approach, this methodology can be successfully scaled to high-dimensional datasets like MIMIC IV-ED, yielding slightly higher clinical utility across decision thresholds compared to baseline models, and reducing the final parameters in the model (Table~\ref{tab:varsel:summary}).

\subsection{Interpretation of Results}

The results highlight that the ``best'' model in purely statistical terms (e.g., maximizing AUC or using AIC/BIC) is not necessarily the most useful model in clinical practice. Purely statistical metrics cannot correctly balance identified cases and the harms of misclassification in the way that \gls{NB} does. In addition, our approach explicitly accounts for harms of measuring predictors as a crucial layer for variable selection. For example, in our simulated illustration, ignoring test harms led to selection of predictors that yielded negligible \gls{NB} improvements at a disproportionate measurement cost. By translating clinical burden into a TT (the minimum number of tests a clinician is willing to perform per true positive to justify the cost), our algorithm penalizes ``expensive'' predictors. Consequently, models developed with this algorithm are naturally more parsimonious and tailored for pragmatic clinical implementation, as seen in the DeNTS trial where the NB-based model achieved a better median \gls{NB} in the 25\%--55\% threshold range while requiring fewer predictors.

\subsection{Contextualization Within Existing Literature}

There is growing recognition in the field of clinical prediction modelling that traditional performance measures are insufficient to guarantee a model's clinical value \cite{steyerberg2019clinical,vancalsterPerformanceEvaluationPredictive2024}. However, embedding utility directly into the variable selection phase has historically been difficult. While Baker et al. (2012) \cite{bakerEvaluatingNewMarker2012} discussed the use of \gls{NB} to evaluate new markers, it was not operationalized as an automated variable selection algorithm. Preliminary efforts to add utility to variable selection have struggled with the generation of utility values and the complexity of the methods \cite{fouskakisBayesianVariableSelection2009}. Fouskakis and colleagues proposed using stochastic optimization to select the model with the best cost-adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). The cost-adjusted BIC extends standard model selection by penalizing the total measurement cost of predictors rather than solely the number of variables included. However, implementing this method requires the specification of a cost-penalized prior, a task that proves difficult for clinicians who must quantify the trade-off between statistical gain and data collection burden. Zhao and Yu developed a cost-sensitive variable selection method based on the $l_{2,1}$-norm. Their work is mainly methodological, without linking it to underlying utility of predictors. The \texttt{glmnet} package in R allows to use weights that can represent misclassification costs. The misclassification costs are used to estimate the model coefficients, therefore providing miscalibrated models, similar to class imbalance corrections. The package also allows to determine penalty factors (i.e. predictor costs). The predictor's cost in the package can only be determined in relative terms without clinical translation, and they do not provide a combined cost approach which is common in medical settings. Our approach bridges this methodological gap by using \enquote{simple} metrics such as \gls{NB} and the TT that have a clear clinical meaning, providing a much more intuitive scale for clinicians to quantify the harm-to-benefit ratio.

\subsection{Limitations}

Despite its advantages, this methodology has several limitations. First, determining the specific \enquote{harm} of measuring a predictor is subjective, highly context-dependent, and challenging to quantify precisely prior to model development (Box 2). There might be situations where the harm of measuring a predictor is established and we advise therefore to use the costs. However, for most situations where this is not possible, the algorithm can be safely used without costs and provide a variable importance metric based on \gls{NB}. Second, this is a data-driven method and therefore it is prone to overfit if sample size is limited. We recommend using the algorithm at least with the minimum sample size recommended by Riley's method \cite{rileyMinimumSampleSize2021}. Third, the exhaustive evaluation of all predictor combinations is computationally intensive and becomes infeasible with many candidate predictors. This is a process that should be done only once for model development, and we provide an option to parallelize the process to reduce considerably the running time. Lastly, we only developed the variable selection methodology for logistic regression, but we aim to increase to other modelling algorithms as part of the \texttt{NBvarsel} package.

\subsection{Implications}

Prediction models that require burdensome, invasive, or expensive data collection for only marginal statistical gains are rarely adopted in routine care \cite{wyattCommentaryPrognosticModels1995}. By integrating clinical utility directly into the model training pipeline, developers can ensure that their final models are both statistically rigorous and practically viable. The open-source \texttt{NBvarsel} R package (\url{https://lasaibarrenada.github.io/NB_varsel/}) and its associated visualizations provide researchers with accessible tools to make informed, utility-driven decisions about variable selection.

\subsection{Future Research}

Future research should prioritize extensive, controlled evaluations of this methodology. Specifically, simulation studies are needed to determine the sample sizes required to prevent overfitting, compare the relative performance of exhaustive versus groupwise approaches, and assess the impact of threshold mismatches between training and testing phases. We also plan to expand the methodology's analytical capabilities. For the current binary logistic regression framework, future updates will introduce full control over variable interactions and spline terms. Furthermore, while we currently use the average \gls{NB} across a reasonable threshold range, we aim to implement kernel smoothing to dynamically weight specific target thresholds. Another related approach to using clinical utility directly into parameter estimation would involve using harm-adjusted \gls{NB} as the optimization criterion for a loss function in deep learning architectures \cite{vickersHypothesisNetBenefit2025}. Although the algorithm's fundamental rationale applies to any prediction model, the current software is limited to binary logistic regression; future iterations will support survival and time-to-event outcomes, alongside other modelling algorithms. Finally, this package is open-source and intended for continuous development, and we actively welcome contributions from the research community.

\subsection{Conclusion}

Clinical utility should not be an afterthought reserved only for external validation. By integrating a harm-adjusted \gls{NB} metric directly into the variable selection process, researchers can produce prediction models that maximize patient benefit while minimizing unnecessary clinical burden.
