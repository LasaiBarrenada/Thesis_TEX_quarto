This thesis set out to improve evidence-informed decision-making in the diagnosis of ovarian cancer by evaluating the \gls{ADNEX} model and advancing statistical methodology for clinical prediction models. The work integrates an applied arm --- focused on evaluating, comparing and updating \gls{ADNEX} in diverse clinical settings --- with a methodological arm that addresses fundamental challenges in model development, evaluation and probability estimation. This chapter synthesises the main findings, discusses related research, acknowledges limitations, and outlines future directions.

\section{Summary of findings}

Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_ADNEXSR} presented the first comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis of \gls{ADNEX} external validation studies. Across 47 studies encompassing more than 17\,000 tumours, the model demonstrated robust discriminative performance, with a summary \gls{AUC} of 0.93 for distinguishing benign from malignant masses \cite{barrenadaADNEXRiskPrediction2024}. However, the review also exposed important weaknesses in the validation literature: 91\% of validations were at high risk of bias, primarily due to small sample sizes, inadequate handling of missing data, and incomplete performance assessment. Perhaps most critically, only 4 of 47 studies reported any form of calibration assessment, making it impossible to draw conclusions about the accuracy of \gls{ADNEX}'s predicted probabilities across settings.

Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_ADNEXvsRMI} extended this work with a head-to-head meta-analysis comparing \gls{ADNEX} to the \gls{RMI}, the most widely used alternative in clinical practice. The results were unequivocal: \gls{ADNEX} consistently outperformed \gls{RMI} in terms of discrimination (summary \gls{AUC} 0.92 vs 0.85 in operated patients) and clinical utility. The probability of \gls{ADNEX} being clinically useful in a hypothetical new centre was 96\%, compared to only 15\% for \gls{RMI} at the 10\% risk threshold \cite{barrenadaHeadHeadComparison2025}. These findings provide strong evidence that clinical guidelines should recommend \gls{ADNEX} over \gls{RMI} for the preoperative characterisation of ovarian masses \cite{sundarRiskpredictionModelsPostmenopausal2024,sundarDiagnosticTestsOvarian2026}.

A recurring theme throughout this thesis is that calibration --- the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed event rates --- remains underappreciated in the clinical prediction literature. The systematic reviews in Chapters~\ref{sec:chp_ADNEXSR} and~\ref{sec:chp_ADNEXvsRMI} showed that most validation studies of \gls{ADNEX} reported discrimination and classification but ignored calibration. That imbalance is methodologically weak and clinically risky. A model can rank patients correctly and still provide systematically wrong absolute probabilities, which can be harmful when the output is used to decide whether a woman with an adnexal mass should be referred, conservatively managed, or operated on \cite{vancalsterCalibrationRiskPrediction2015}. In the context of \gls{ADNEX} and prediction models as decision support tools, calibration and the probability estimate is not a secondary output: it is the output that informs action.

The same applied story motivates Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_ADNEX2}, which is still ongoing. The original \gls{ADNEX} development data were necessarily limited to women who underwent surgery, whereas in clinical practice the model is intended to be used to determine who to operate. We used bayesian refitting borrowing information from the original model to update \gls{ADNEX} with a larger and more representative dataset that includes non-operated patients. The goal is to produce  the \emph{ADNEX2} model that is better calibrated and more robust for use in a two-step strategy that starts with the simple benign descriptors, followed by the full model for indeterminate cases.

Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_RFOverfitting} shifted attention from the applied model to the behaviour of algorithms used for probability estimation. Using both a case study in ovarian malignancy prediction and simulation experiments, the chapter showed that random forests can achieve almost perfect training \gls{AUC}s, which is often a sign of overfitting, and at the same time competitive test performance. The key issue is not classical overfitting in the narrow sense, but the tendency of fully grown trees to create local probability peaks and unstable risk estimates. That distinction matters because prediction models are often tuned as if classification performance were the only objective. For risk prediction, however, the quality of the probabilities themselves is central, and the default strategy of growing trees as deep as possible can be counterproductive when the goal is calibration rather than only discrimination. 

Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_ClusteredCalibration} addressed a different but closely related issue: calibration should not be treated as a purely pooled property when the data come from multiple centres. Much of the evidence used to evaluate clinical prediction models is clustered by hospital or study, yet traditional calibration plots suppress that structure. The chapter therefore proposed clustered group calibration (CG-C), two-stage meta-analysis calibration (2MA-C), and mixed model calibration (MIX-C). The main lesson is that calibration has both an overall component and a between-centre component. The recommended approaches, especially 2MA-C with splines and MIX-C, make that distinction visible by combining an overall calibration curve with prediction intervals where the calibration of a new centre should lie. That is a more realistic summary than a single pooled curve because it asks the clinically relevant question of whether a model remains well calibrated when it is moved to a new setting.

Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_FundamentalProblem} took a step back from the technical evaluation of model performance to examine a more fundamental question: how much can we trust a risk estimate for an individual patient? The study demonstrated that individual risk estimates are far more uncertain than commonly assumed. While estimation uncertainty (due to finite sample size) decreases with larger training samples, model uncertainty (due to arbitrary modelling choices) and applicability uncertainty (due to differences in measurement procedures and populations) remain substantial. Even with 10\,000 training observations, the 95\% range of predicted probabilities for the same patient averaged 0.39 on the probability scale. These findings challenge the common practice of presenting a single risk estimate to patients and clinicians without acknowledging the underlying uncertainty.

Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_varselection} is still under development, but it follows naturally from the methodological chapters. If a model is judged not only by fit but also by usefulness, then predictor selection should account for the clinical consequences of including or excluding variables. That question is especially relevant in settings such as ovarian mass diagnosis, where a model may guide referral, surgery, or follow-up decisions.





\section{From population performance to individual decisions}
Clinical prediction models are developed and evaluated at the population level, but their purpose is to inform decisions for individual patients \cite{steyerbergClinicalPredictionModels2009}. The chapters in this thesis show that moving from population performance to individual decision-making adds successive layers of uncertainty. Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_ADNEXSR} established that \gls{ADNEX} performs well on average across settings. Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_ADNEXvsRMI} showed that it compares favourably with \gls{RMI} for triage. Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_ClusteredCalibration} showed that pooled performance can mask meaningful centre-to-centre variation. Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_FundamentalProblem} then showed that uncertainty persists even after all of those population-level summaries are taken into account.

That sequence matters because the apparent precision of a risk estimate can be misleading. A patient and clinician may see one number, but that number is the product of a model that may behave differently in another centre, another patient subgroup, or another data collection context. Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_ClusteredCalibration} makes this visible at the centre level; Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_FundamentalProblem} makes it visible at the individual level. 
Chapters~\ref{sec:chp_ADNEXSR} and~\ref{sec:chp_ADNEXvsRMI} established that \gls{ADNEX} performs well on average across diverse populations. However, both chapters noted considerable heterogeneity in performance across centres and studies. Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_ClusteredCalibration} provided formal methods to quantify this heterogeneity, showing that calibration can vary substantially between clusters even when the overall curve appears satisfactory. 
This has direct implications for the concept of \gls{EIDM} introduced earlier in the thesis. A predicted probability should be treated as an informed estimate, not as a precise measurement. In practice, that means risk estimates should be communicated together with an explanation of what is known, what is uncertain, and why the estimate may change if the patient were seen in another centre or assessed under slightly different assumptions \cite{pateUncertaintyUsingRisk2019, rileyUncertaintyRiskEstimates2025}.
Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_FundamentalProblem} pushed this line of reasoning further, showing that even within a single centre, the predicted probability for an individual patient is subject to model and applicability uncertainty that cannot be reduced by increasing sample size alone \cite{pateUncertaintyUsingRisk2019, rileyUncertaintyRiskEstimates2025}. This has direct implications for the concept of \gls{EIDM} discussed in the introduction: a risk estimate of 15\% should not be interpreted as a precise measurement but rather as a point within a range of plausible values, each of which might lead to a different clinical decision.
These findings do not diminish the value of clinical prediction models. \gls{ADNEX} remains a useful tool for ovarian mass characterisation because it outperforms its main comparator and can support referral and management decisions. The lesson is instead that its outputs should be used with methodological humility. Clinical prediction models should support shared decision-making rather than replace it, and that requires a more explicit discussion of uncertainty than is common in routine practice \cite{moonsCriticalAppraisalData2014, steyerbergClinicalPredictionModels2009}.
These findings do not diminish the value of clinical prediction models. \gls{ADNEX} remains a valuable tool for informing the management of ovarian masses, as demonstrated by its superior performance compared to alternatives. However, they argue for a more nuanced approach to communicating model outputs --- one that acknowledges uncertainty and supports shared decision-making between clinicians and patients, rather than treating predicted probabilities as definitive answers \cite{moonsCriticalAppraisalData2014, steyerbergClinicalPredictionModels2009}.

\section{Quality of the published evidence}

The systematic reviews in Chapters~\ref{sec:chp_ADNEXSR} and~\ref{sec:chp_ADNEXvsRMI} consistently identified poor methodological quality in external validation studies. The most common issues --- assessed using the \gls{PROBAST} tool \cite{wolffPROBASTToolAssess2019} --- were inadequate sample sizes, inappropriate exclusion of patients with missing data, and incomplete reporting of model performance. Adherence to the \gls{TRIPOD} reporting guidelines \cite{collinsTransparentReportingMultivariable2015} was low, with key items such as sample size justification, missing data handling, and calibration assessment reported in fewer than 8\% of studies. This findings are consistent with previous reviews of clinical prediction model validation studies, which have repeatedly highlighted similar issues \cite{collinsExternalValidationMultivariable2014, bouwmeesterReportingMethodsClinical2012, andaurnavarroCompletenessReportingClinical2022,andaurnavarroSystematicReviewFinds2023, andaurnavarroSystematicReviewIdentifies2023, dhimanMethodologicalConductPrognostic2022, dhimanOverinterpretationFindingsMachine2023}.

These findings are concerning in light of the regulatory developments discussed in the introduction. As clinical prediction models increasingly fall under medical device regulation --- including the EU \gls{MDR} and the \gls{SaMD} framework --- the evidence base for their validation must improve. Regulatory frameworks require demonstration of safety and effectiveness, which in the context of prediction models translates to robust evidence of discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility across diverse populations \cite{vancalsterPerformanceEvaluationPredictive2024, collinsTRIPODAIStatementUpdated2024}. The current state of the validation literature and regulatory approval, falls short of what should be expected for tools that are embedded in clinical practice. Recent studies have highlighted that many models that have been approved and are in use have not undergone rigorous external validation, and their performance in real-world settings is often unknown \cite{mehtaEvaluatingTransparencyAI2025,vanleeuwenArtificialIntelligenceRadiology2021}.

Improving this situation requires a combination of better study design (adequate sample sizes \cite{rileyMinimumSampleSize2021}, proper missing data handling, prospective data collection), more complete reporting (adherence to \gls{TRIPOD} and its extensions \cite{debrayTransparentReportingMultivariable2023, snellTransparentReportingMultivariable2023}), and a shift in culture towards recognising external validation as an ongoing process rather than a one-time exercise \cite{vancalsterThereNoSuch2023}.

\section{Limitations and ongoing work}
Several limitations should be acknowledged at the thesis level, beyond those discussed in individual chapters.
Several limitations should be acknowledged at the thesis level, beyond those discussed in individual chapters.
First, the applied work focuses on \gls{ADNEX} and ovarian mass characterisation. That focus is a strength because it allows the methodological questions to be studied in a clinically meaningful setting, but it also means that the applied conclusions should not be overstated beyond this domain. The methodological chapters are more general in intent, yet they were illustrated mainly using one clinical problem with a moderately common outcome. Their behaviour in rarer diseases, different predictor structures, or weaker models remains to be studied.
First, the applied work focuses exclusively on the \gls{ADNEX} model and the clinical domain of ovarian mass characterisation. While the methodological contributions --- clustered calibration plots, the analysis of random forest probability estimation, and the uncertainty framework --- are designed to be general, their empirical illustration is drawn from a single clinical domain with a relatively common outcome (approximately 37\% malignancy rate among operated patients). The behaviour of these methods and findings in settings with rarer outcomes, different predictor types, or weaker models remains to be evaluated.
Second, two studies are still ongoing. Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_varselection} is intended to develop a variable selection strategy that optimises clinical utility rather than purely statistical fit, which would extend the thesis from model assessment into model construction. Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_ADNEX2} is aimed at updating \gls{ADNEX} with newer data, including non-operated patients, so that the model better reflects contemporary clinical practice. Until those studies are completed, the full cycle of evaluate, understand, update, and re-validate remains unfinished.
Second, two planned chapters are not yet completed. A study on variable selection using net benefit as the optimisation criterion (Chapter 05.06) aims to develop methods that prioritise clinical utility over purely statistical fit during model development. This addresses a gap identified across the thesis: current variable selection methods optimise for discrimination or overall fit, but the ultimate goal of a clinical prediction model is to improve patient outcomes. Additionally, the development of \gls{ADNEX}2 (Chapter 05.07) --- an updated version of the \gls{ADNEX} model incorporating data from non-operated patients, new \gls{IOTA} studies, and the methodological insights gained throughout this thesis --- is ongoing. Until these chapters are completed, the full applied cycle of evaluate $\rightarrow$ understand $\rightarrow$ update $\rightarrow$ re-validate remains open.
Third, most analyses in this thesis rely on retrospective or secondary analyses of existing datasets. Even where the underlying data collection was prospective and standardised, the specific meta-analytic comparisons and methodological evaluations were not originally the primary purpose of data collection. Prospective validation of the updated \gls{ADNEX}2 model will therefore be essential.
Third, most analyses in this thesis rely on retrospective data. While the \gls{IOTA} data collection follows standardised protocols and prospective designs, the meta-analyses in Chapters~\ref{sec:chp_ADNEXSR} and~\ref{sec:chp_ADNEXvsRMI} synthesise studies that were not originally designed for the specific comparisons performed. Prospective evaluation of the updated \gls{ADNEX}2 model in new clinical settings will be an essential step.
Fourth, the clustered calibration methods developed in Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_ClusteredCalibration} were evaluated under relatively simple simulation scenarios. More complex forms of heterogeneity, including \glspl{random_slope} and nonlinear centre-specific deviations, were not explored exhaustively. The prediction intervals are an important step forward, but they are not a final solution to the problem of quantifying between-centre uncertainty.

More complex forms of between-centre variation, such as \glspl{random_slope} or nonlinear centre-specific deviations, were not systematically explored. \section{Future directions}

The findings of this thesis point towards several directions for future work.

\subsection{Updating ADNEX}
The most immediate applied priority is the development and validation of \gls{ADNEX}2. The preceding chapters make clear what such an update should address. The systematic reviews identified heterogeneity across studies and centres; the comparison with \gls{RMI} confirmed that \gls{ADNEX} should remain the reference point for ovarian mass triage; and the uncertainty framework showed that the model should not be presented as more precise than it really is. An updated model should therefore incorporate non-operated patients, include more recent \gls{IOTA} data, and be evaluated with methods that explicitly quantify calibration in clustered settings \cite{vancalsterEvaluatingRiskOvarian2014, steyerbergAssessmentHeterogeneityIndividual2019}.
The most immediate applied priority is the development and validation of \gls{ADNEX}2. The systematic reviews identified sources of heterogeneity and limitations of the current model that should guide the update. Key improvements include expanding the development data to include non-operated patients (addressing the selection bias of the original model \cite{vancalsterEvaluatingRiskOvarian2014}), incorporating more recent \gls{IOTA} data, and evaluating whether additional or different predictors can reduce between-centre heterogeneity \cite{steyerbergAssessmentHeterogeneityIndividual2019}. The updated model should be validated using the clustered calibration methods from Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_ClusteredCalibration} to ensure that calibration is satisfactory not only on average but also across individual centres.

\subsection{Variable selection for clinical utility}
Current variable selection methods in prediction modelling typically optimise for statistical criteria such as the Akaike Information Criterion, cross-validated log-loss, or discrimination. Yet the practical goal of a diagnostic model is not statistical parsimony for its own sake, but better decisions. Building variable selection procedures around net benefit or related utility measures could produce models that are simpler, more transparent, and more clinically relevant, even if they trade a small amount of traditional statistical performance for decision value \cite{vickersNetBenefitApproaches2016}. That is the natural direction of Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_varselection}.
Current variable selection methods in prediction modelling typically optimise for statistical criteria such as the Akaike Information Criterion, cross-validated log-loss, or discrimination. However, the ultimate purpose of a diagnostic prediction model is to improve clinical decisions. Developing variable selection algorithms that directly optimise for net benefit \cite{vickersNetBenefitApproaches2016} or related clinical utility measures could lead to simpler models that are more useful in practice, even if they sacrifice some statistical performance. This is the focus of the ongoing work in Chapter 05.06.

\subsection{Extending clustered calibration methodology}
The methods proposed in Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_ClusteredCalibration} can be extended in several directions. Bayesian approaches to prediction interval estimation may improve coverage properties, and more complex heterogeneity structures should be evaluated. It would also be useful to apply the methods beyond logistic regression, especially to machine learning models whose calibration behaviour may differ in important ways from that of classical parametric models.
The methods proposed in Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_ClusteredCalibration} can be extended in several directions. Bayesian approaches to prediction interval estimation may improve coverage properties compared to the current frequentist methods. More complex heterogeneity structures, including \glspl{random_slope} and nonlinear centre effects, should be evaluated. The methods should also be tested with prediction models beyond logistic regression, including machine learning algorithms whose probability estimates may exhibit different calibration patterns.

\subsection{Uncertainty communication in clinical practice}
Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_FundamentalProblem} demonstrated that individual risk estimates carry substantial uncertainty, but translating that insight into clinical practice remains an open challenge. Future work should examine how to communicate that uncertainty without making prediction models unusable or untrusted. Possible approaches include presenting ranges rather than single numbers, using prediction abstention when uncertainty is excessive, and incorporating uncertainty information into decision support systems \cite{kompaSecondOpinionNeeded2021}. These developments would bring the technical work of this thesis closer to the broader goals of \gls{EIDM} and shared decision-making.

Future work should explore how uncertainty can be communicated effectively to clinicians and patients without undermining trust in prediction models \cite{kompaSecondOpinionNeeded2021}. Practical approaches may include prediction abstention (withholding a risk estimate when uncertainty is too high), presenting ranges instead of point estimates, and integrating uncertainty quantification into clinical decision support systems. These developments connect directly to the broader goal of \gls{EIDM} and shared decision-making.

\subsection{Model lifecycle monitoring}
As clinical prediction models are increasingly recognised as medical devices, continuous monitoring of their performance after deployment becomes a regulatory and ethical requirement. Methods for detecting performance degradation over time --- due to changes in patient populations, clinical practice, or measurement technologies --- therefore need to be developed and standardised. The clustered calibration methods in Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_ClusteredCalibration} provide a foundation for that task, but further work is needed to adapt them for sequential surveillance rather than one-time validation.

\section{Conclusion}
This thesis contributes to the development and evaluation of clinical prediction models for ovarian cancer diagnosis from both applied and methodological perspectives. The systematic evaluation of \gls{ADNEX} confirmed its value as a diagnostic tool and provided the evidence base for recommending it over alternatives such as the \gls{RMI}. At the same time, the methodological chapters showed why this should not be the end of the story: calibration is rarely assessed, probability estimates from machine learning algorithms can be misleading, clustering changes how performance should be interpreted, and individual risk predictions carry more uncertainty than is often acknowledged.
This thesis contributes to the development and evaluation of clinical prediction models for ovarian cancer diagnosis from both applied and methodological perspectives. The systematic evaluation of \gls{ADNEX} confirmed its value as a diagnostic tool and provided the evidence base for recommending it over alternatives such as the \gls{RMI}. At the same time, the methodological work revealed important challenges: calibration is rarely assessed, probability estimates from machine learning algorithms can be misleading, accounting for clustering is essential for realistic performance assessment, and individual risk predictions carry more uncertainty than is commonly acknowledged.
These findings underscore that clinical prediction models, including \gls{ADNEX}, are valuable but imperfect tools. Their responsible use requires rigorous validation, transparent communication of uncertainty, and ongoing monitoring --- principles that are increasingly formalised in both reporting guidelines and medical device regulation. The ongoing work on variable selection for clinical utility and the \gls{ADNEX}2 update aims to close the applied cycle by translating the methodological insights of this thesis into a better model for clinical practice \cite{dejongDevelopingMoreGeneralizable2021}.
