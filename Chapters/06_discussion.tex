This thesis set out to enhance evidence-informed decision-making in the diagnosis of ovarian cancer by evaluating the \gls{ADNEX} model and advancing statistical methodology for clinical prediction models. The work integrates an applied arm --- focused on evaluating and comparing \gls{ADNEX} in diverse clinical settings --- with a methodological arm that addresses fundamental challenges in model evaluation and probability estimation. This chapter synthesises the main findings, discusses cross-cutting themes, acknowledges limitations, and outlines future directions including two ongoing studies on variable selection with clinical utility criteria and the development of an updated \gls{ADNEX} model (\gls{ADNEX}2).

\section{Summary of findings}

\subsection{Applied contributions: evaluating ADNEX}

Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_ADNEXSR} presented the first comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis of \gls{ADNEX} external validation studies. Across 47 studies encompassing more than 17\,000 tumours, the model demonstrated robust discriminative performance, with a summary \gls{AUC} of 0.93 for distinguishing benign from malignant masses \cite{barrenadaADNEXRiskPrediction2024}. However, the review also exposed important weaknesses in the validation literature: 91\% of validations were at high risk of bias, primarily due to small sample sizes, inadequate handling of missing data, and incomplete performance assessment. Perhaps most critically, only 4 of 47 studies reported any form of calibration assessment, making it impossible to draw conclusions about the accuracy of \gls{ADNEX}'s predicted probabilities across settings.

Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_ADNEXvsRMI} extended this work with a head-to-head meta-analysis comparing \gls{ADNEX} to the \gls{RMI}, the most widely used alternative in clinical practice. The results were unequivocal: \gls{ADNEX} consistently outperformed \gls{RMI} in terms of discrimination (summary \gls{AUC} 0.92 vs 0.85 in operated patients) and clinical utility. The probability of \gls{ADNEX} being clinically useful in a hypothetical new centre was 96\%, compared to only 15\% for \gls{RMI} at the 10\% risk threshold \cite{barrenadaHeadHeadComparison2025}. These findings provide strong evidence that clinical guidelines should recommend \gls{ADNEX} over \gls{RMI} for the preoperative characterisation of ovarian masses.

\subsection{Methodological contributions}

Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_RFOverfitting} investigated the behaviour of random forests for probability estimation, a topic that had received little attention compared to their use for classification. Using both a case study on ovarian malignancy prediction and simulation experiments, the study showed that random forests achieve near-perfect training \gls{AUC}s not through classical overfitting but by learning local probability peaks in the data space. This phenomenon, driven by the use of fully grown trees, means that training \gls{AUC} is uninformative about test performance for random forests. Crucially, for probability estimation --- as opposed to classification --- the common recommendation to use fully grown trees is counterproductive \cite{hastieElementsStatisticalLearning2001a}: it leads to miscalibrated predicted probabilities with too little spread.

Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_ClusteredCalibration} addressed a gap in the methodology for calibration assessment. Most clinical prediction models are developed and validated using multicenter data \cite{wynantsUntappedPotentialMulticenter2019}, yet no methods existed to construct calibration plots that account for clustering. The chapter proposed three approaches: clustered group calibration (CGC), two-stage meta-analysis calibration (TMAC), and mixed model calibration (MIXC). The recommended approach --- TMAC with splines --- provides both an overall calibration curve and 95\% prediction intervals that quantify between-centre heterogeneity, enabling researchers to assess whether a model's calibration generalises to new centres. These methods were implemented in the CalibrationCurves R package, making them directly available to applied researchers.

Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_FundamentalProblem} took a step back from the technical evaluation of model performance to examine a more fundamental question: how much can we trust a risk estimate for an individual patient? The study demonstrated that individual risk estimates are far more uncertain than commonly assumed. While estimation uncertainty (due to finite sample size) decreases with larger training samples, model uncertainty (due to arbitrary modelling choices) and applicability uncertainty (due to differences in measurement procedures and populations) remain substantial. Even with 10\,000 training observations, the 95\% range of predicted probabilities for the same patient averaged 0.39 on the probability scale. These findings challenge the common practice of presenting a single risk estimate to patients and clinicians without acknowledging the underlying uncertainty.

\section{Calibration: the underappreciated dimension}

A recurring theme throughout this thesis is that calibration --- the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed event rates --- remains critically underappreciated in the prediction modelling literature. The systematic reviews in Chapters~\ref{sec:chp_ADNEXSR} and~\ref{sec:chp_ADNEXvsRMI} found that the vast majority of \gls{ADNEX} validation studies focused exclusively on discrimination, neglecting calibration entirely. This is problematic because a model can have excellent discrimination while producing systematically inaccurate probabilities \cite{vancalsterCalibrationRiskPrediction2015}. For \gls{ADNEX}, where the predicted probabilities directly inform clinical decision-making about surgical management, calibration is arguably more important than discrimination \cite{vancalsterCalibrationHierarchyRisk2016}.

Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_RFOverfitting} illustrated that calibration problems can arise at the algorithmic level. Random forests, when used with default settings, produce predicted probabilities with insufficient spread, resulting in calibration slopes consistently above 1. This finding has practical implications for the growing number of studies that apply machine learning algorithms to clinical prediction problems without carefully evaluating calibration \cite{dhimanReportingPrognosticClinical2021, andaurnavarroRiskBiasStudies2021}.

Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_ClusteredCalibration} provided the methodological tools needed to assess calibration properly in the multicenter settings where models like \gls{ADNEX} are evaluated. By quantifying between-centre heterogeneity in calibration through prediction intervals, these methods make it possible to answer a clinically relevant question: if I deploy this model in a new centre, how well calibrated is it likely to be?

Finally, Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_FundamentalProblem} showed that even if a model achieves good population-level calibration, the individual predicted probability remains highly uncertain. This places an upper bound on the confidence with which any single risk estimate should be communicated or acted upon.

Taken together, these chapters make the case that calibration should be central to the evaluation of clinical prediction models --- from the choice of algorithm and hyperparameters, through multicenter validation, to the communication of risk to patients \cite{vancalsterPerformanceEvaluationPredictive2024}.

\section{From population performance to individual decisions}

Clinical prediction models are developed and evaluated at the population level, but their ultimate purpose is to inform decisions for individual patients \cite{steyerbergClinicalPredictionModels2009}. This thesis traces a progression from population-level evaluation to individual-level uncertainty, revealing increasing layers of complexity at each step.

Chapters~\ref{sec:chp_ADNEXSR} and~\ref{sec:chp_ADNEXvsRMI} established that \gls{ADNEX} performs well on average across diverse populations. However, both chapters noted considerable heterogeneity in performance across centres and studies. Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_ClusteredCalibration} provided formal methods to quantify this heterogeneity, showing that calibration can vary substantially between clusters even when the overall curve appears satisfactory --- a form of Simpson's paradox.

Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_FundamentalProblem} pushed this line of reasoning further, demonstrating that even within a single centre, the predicted probability for an individual patient is subject to model and applicability uncertainty that cannot be reduced by increasing sample size alone \cite{pateUncertaintyUsingRisk2019, rileyUncertaintyRiskEstimates2025}. This has direct implications for the concept of \gls{EIDM} discussed in the introduction: a risk estimate of 15\% should not be interpreted as a precise measurement but rather as a point within a range of plausible values, each of which might lead to a different clinical decision.

These findings do not diminish the value of clinical prediction models. \gls{ADNEX} remains a valuable tool for informing the management of ovarian masses, as demonstrated by its superior performance compared to alternatives. However, they argue for a more nuanced approach to communicating model outputs --- one that acknowledges uncertainty and supports shared decision-making between clinicians and patients, rather than treating predicted probabilities as definitive answers \cite{moonsCriticalAppraisalData2014, steyerbergClinicalPredictionModels2009}.

\section{Methodological quality of external validation studies}

The systematic reviews in Chapters~\ref{sec:chp_ADNEXSR} and~\ref{sec:chp_ADNEXvsRMI} consistently identified poor methodological quality in external validation studies. The most common issues --- assessed using the \gls{PROBAST} tool \cite{wolffPROBASTToolAssess2019} --- were inadequate sample sizes, inappropriate exclusion of patients with missing data, and incomplete reporting of model performance. Adherence to the \gls{TRIPOD} reporting guidelines \cite{collinsTransparentReportingMultivariable2015} was low, with key items such as sample size justification, missing data handling, and calibration assessment reported in fewer than 35\% of studies.

These findings are concerning in light of the regulatory developments discussed in the introduction. As clinical prediction models increasingly fall under medical device regulation --- including the EU \gls{MDR} and the \gls{SaMD} framework --- the evidence base for their validation must improve. Regulatory frameworks require demonstration of safety and effectiveness, which in the context of prediction models translates to robust evidence of discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility across diverse populations \cite{vancalsterPerformanceEvaluationPredictive2024, collinsTRIPODAIStatementUpdated2024}. The current state of the validation literature, at least for \gls{ADNEX}, falls short of what should be expected for a tool that is embedded in clinical guidelines and ultrasound devices worldwide \cite{collinsExternalValidationMultivariable2014, bouwmeesterReportingMethodsClinical2012}.

Improving this situation requires a combination of better study design (adequate sample sizes \cite{rileyMinimumSampleSize2021}, proper missing data handling, prospective data collection), more complete reporting (adherence to \gls{TRIPOD} and its extensions \cite{debrayTransparentReportingMultivariable2023, snellTransparentReportingMultivariable2023}), and a shift in culture towards recognising external validation as an ongoing process rather than a one-time exercise \cite{vancalsterThereNoSuch2023}.

\section{Limitations and ongoing work}

Several limitations should be acknowledged at the thesis level, beyond those discussed in individual chapters.

First, the applied work focuses exclusively on the \gls{ADNEX} model and the clinical domain of ovarian mass characterisation. While the methodological contributions --- clustered calibration plots, the analysis of random forest probability estimation, and the uncertainty framework --- are designed to be general, their empirical illustration is drawn from a single clinical domain with a relatively common outcome (approximately 37\% malignancy rate among operated patients). The behaviour of these methods and findings in settings with rarer outcomes, different predictor types, or weaker models remains to be evaluated.

Second, two planned chapters are not yet completed. A study on variable selection using net benefit as the optimisation criterion (Chapter 05.06) aims to develop methods that prioritise clinical utility over purely statistical fit during model development. This addresses a gap identified across the thesis: current variable selection methods optimise for discrimination or overall fit, but the ultimate goal of a clinical prediction model is to improve patient outcomes. Additionally, the development of \gls{ADNEX}2 (Chapter 05.07) --- an updated version of the \gls{ADNEX} model incorporating data from non-operated patients, new \gls{IOTA} studies, and the methodological insights gained throughout this thesis --- is ongoing. Until these chapters are completed, the full applied cycle of evaluate $\rightarrow$ understand $\rightarrow$ update $\rightarrow$ re-validate remains open.

Third, most analyses in this thesis rely on retrospective data. While the \gls{IOTA} data collection follows standardised protocols and prospective designs, the meta-analyses in Chapters~\ref{sec:chp_ADNEXSR} and~\ref{sec:chp_ADNEXvsRMI} synthesise studies that were not originally designed for the specific comparisons performed. Prospective evaluation of the updated \gls{ADNEX}2 model in new clinical settings will be an essential step.

Fourth, the clustered calibration methods developed in Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_ClusteredCalibration} were evaluated in simulation scenarios with relatively simple heterogeneity structures. More complex forms of between-centre variation, such as \glspl{random_slope} or nonlinear centre-specific deviations, were not systematically explored. The prediction intervals, while an important first step, did not achieve nominal coverage in all scenarios.

\section{Future directions}

The findings of this thesis point towards several directions for future work.

\subsection{Updating ADNEX}

The most immediate applied priority is the development and validation of \gls{ADNEX}2. The systematic reviews identified sources of heterogeneity and limitations of the current model that should guide the update. Key improvements include expanding the development data to include non-operated patients (addressing the selection bias of the original model \cite{vancalsterEvaluatingRiskOvarian2014}), incorporating more recent \gls{IOTA} data, and evaluating whether additional or different predictors can reduce between-centre heterogeneity \cite{steyerbergAssessmentHeterogeneityIndividual2019}. The updated model should be validated using the clustered calibration methods from Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_ClusteredCalibration} to ensure that calibration is satisfactory not only on average but also across individual centres.

\subsection{Variable selection for clinical utility}

Current variable selection methods in prediction modelling typically optimise for statistical criteria such as the Akaike Information Criterion, cross-validated log-loss, or discrimination. However, the ultimate purpose of a diagnostic prediction model is to improve clinical decisions. Developing variable selection algorithms that directly optimise for net benefit \cite{vickersNetBenefitApproaches2016} or related clinical utility measures could lead to simpler models that are more useful in practice, even if they sacrifice some statistical performance. This is the focus of the ongoing work in Chapter 05.06.

\subsection{Extending clustered calibration methodology}

The methods proposed in Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_ClusteredCalibration} can be extended in several directions. Bayesian approaches to prediction interval estimation may improve coverage properties compared to the current frequentist methods. More complex heterogeneity structures, including \glspl{random_slope} and nonlinear centre effects, should be evaluated. The methods should also be tested with prediction models beyond logistic regression, including machine learning algorithms whose probability estimates may exhibit different calibration patterns.

\subsection{Uncertainty communication in clinical practice}

Chapter~\ref{sec:chp_FundamentalProblem} demonstrated that individual risk estimates carry substantial uncertainty, but translating this insight into clinical practice remains an open challenge. Future work should explore how uncertainty can be communicated effectively to clinicians and patients without undermining trust in prediction models \cite{kompaSecondOpinionNeeded2021}. Practical approaches may include prediction abstention (withholding a risk estimate when uncertainty is too high), presenting ranges instead of point estimates, and integrating uncertainty quantification into clinical decision support systems. These developments connect directly to the broader goal of \gls{EIDM} and shared decision-making.

\subsection{Model lifecycle monitoring}

As clinical prediction models are increasingly recognised as medical devices, continuous monitoring of their performance after deployment becomes a regulatory and ethical requirement. Methods for detecting performance degradation over time --- due to changes in patient populations, clinical practices, or measurement technologies --- need to be developed and standardised. The clustered calibration methods from this thesis provide a foundation for such monitoring, but further work is needed to adapt them for sequential surveillance rather than one-time validation.

\section{Conclusion}

This thesis contributes to the development and evaluation of clinical prediction models for ovarian cancer diagnosis from both applied and methodological perspectives. The systematic evaluation of \gls{ADNEX} confirmed its value as a diagnostic tool and provided the evidence base for recommending it over alternatives such as the \gls{RMI}. At the same time, the methodological work revealed important challenges: calibration is rarely assessed, probability estimates from machine learning algorithms can be misleading, accounting for clustering is essential for realistic performance assessment, and individual risk predictions carry more uncertainty than is commonly acknowledged.

These findings underscore that clinical prediction models, including \gls{ADNEX}, are valuable but imperfect tools. Their responsible use requires rigorous validation, transparent communication of uncertainty, and ongoing monitoring --- principles that are increasingly formalised in both reporting guidelines and medical device regulation. The ongoing work on variable selection for clinical utility and the \gls{ADNEX}2 update aims to close the applied cycle by translating the methodological insights of this thesis into a better model for clinical practice \cite{dejongDevelopingMoreGeneralizable2021}.
